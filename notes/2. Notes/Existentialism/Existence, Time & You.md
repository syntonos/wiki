# Existence, Time & You 
	
Across these stories, there exist two interpretations of time: time as the inevitable, the dread-inducing linear march from birth to non-existence, and time as a loop, the repetitive, cyclical images that chain existence to structure. By forcing these characters to contend with these temporal patterns, Sartre, Camus, and Hemingway flatten life and death to a single plane, compelling them to look into the “nothingness” at the heart of both. 

Ibietta’s narrative begins with the pronouncement of his death. “You will be executed tomorrow morning,” and the gaping maw of his own mortality stares back. Suddenly, shockingly, Ibietta is forced to contend with time the linear march, his impending non-existence, and life and death start to distort. The artifacts of life bleed out of him, one by one, heat, sensation, and individualism, rendering him an indistinct, pale specter. It is in this space that life and death start to blur, compressed to the same plane, and Ibietta realises the futility death brings. “I had spend my time counterfeiting eternity, I had understood nothing…death had disenchanted everything (Sartre 11)”: it is here where Ibietta, so stuck between life and death, is struck by time’s cruel, inexorable progression, there is no such thing as eternity, and so the futility of death taints all. Ibietta, too, comes to contend with the temporal pattern of time as absurd, endless cycle. He simply cannot experience all of it, but he sees. The absurd cycle of the changing of his fate, his stay of execution and his fugitive’s death, both equally absurd and cyclic. The endless cycles of history, a thousand Francisco Francos, in turns of past and future, an inevitable loop. There is no turning of time that will be different. Futility and absurdity—confronted by both, Ibietta gazes into the “nothingness” at the core of his universe, and it consumes him; his epiphany eats him alive. 

Sisyphus, by contrast, takes the “nothingness” that devours Ibietta and shapes it into something worth pursuing. His story, too, begins with the pronouncement of his death (or really his third). Death is a malleable veil in the myths of old, and by this, Sisyphus’ death and subsequent punishment is more akin to entering limbo, the liminal space between life and death, than true non-existence. Here, time the inevitable, time the linear march pauses; rather what holds dominion is time the infinite loop. Rather than forcing Sisyphus to contend with his own mortality (that’s really the first part of the myth, Camus focuses with the second), as Sartre does Ibietta, Camus is more interested in forcing Sisyphus to stare into the endlessness of eternity, and see the temporal, repetitive cycles that chain his existence to the same task as surely as he is chained to his boulder. Sisyphus is assaulted with both the futility of death and the absurdity of life, forced to see the “nothingness” at the core of his existence. And yet, instead of falling into despair as Ibietta does, he finds himself happy. As Camus writes, “happiness and the absurd are two sons of the same earth. They are inseparable.” (Camus 1). Sisyphus must peer into nothing to find the truth of what makes him content. He sees the higher temporal patterns of eternity, and he rejects them. His fate has nothing to do with these cyclical chains of time, but rather it is what he makes of it—he “makes of fate a human matter.” (Camus 1). He peers into the temporal patterns of the world, the nothingness at the heart of the universe, and he imagines himself happy.

A Clean, Well-Lighted Place begins, too, with the pronouncement of death. It’s failed, of course, but that’s not what’s relevant. The old man has peered into the emptiness of the universe and felt likely the same despair Ibietta does; all he wants now is the escape of a true death. The waiters consider his attempted suicide: Hemingway forces the two to contend with time the linear, the inevitable march towards non-existence. Yet he also forces them to contend with time the repetitive, endless cycle, the threat of capitalism chains them to the same shifts of their work, day by day by day, as surely as Sisyphus’ chains bind him to his boulder. The younger waiter does not yet peer into the nothingness this awareness of time demands—he has not yet gained awareness, he is young, yet, with a family to live for. The older waiter is who Hemingway forces to contend the nothing at the core of his existence. “‘You have everything I have.’ ‘No, I have never had confidence, and I am not young.” (Hemingway 2). The older waiter has nothing beyond this job, nothing beyond the endless loop of this cafe and the suffocating awareness of his own mortality. Suffused with this dual awareness, however, he is remarkably undespairing. He finds solace in “nothingness”. Emptiness and order, the physical manifestations of nothing, are what he finds value in. He clutches to his existence as a bastion of cleanliness and light. Consumed by nothingness, he allows it to give him meaning. 
	
Each of these stories forces their heroes to peer into the futility of death, the absurdity of life, and the “nothingness” that these temporal patterns reveal. Yet instead of being consumed by despair, so many of them find themselves happy; and so perhaps the lesson is simply this, live, dance while you can, and imagine yourself happy.
